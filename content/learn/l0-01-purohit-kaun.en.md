# The word and the person

The Sanskrit word *purohita* means "one placed in front": the person a household puts forward to conduct its rites on its behalf. In the Hindi belt the family simply says [[pandit]], and the handbooks say [[acharya]] for the priest who leads a rite. The family for whom the rite is performed is the [[yajman]]; the person who actually makes the offerings — the father of the bride at a wedding, the eldest son at a shraddha — is the [[karta]]. A purohit therefore does not perform the rite *instead of* the family; he conducts it *for* them, speaking the mantras and guiding every action while the host makes the [[sankalp]], the offerings and the gifts.

Many families have a kul-purohit whose link with the household goes back generations; many others call a pandit from a temple, a Sanskrit school or a neighbour's recommendation for each occasion. Both are normal today.

# What the purohit actually does

In every rite on this site the purohit's work has the same five parts:

1. **Knows the sequence** — which step comes after which, and why. The order of a wedding, a havan or a shraddha is fixed by the sutra and the [[paddhati]]; the pandit carries it in his [[pothi]] and in memory.
2. **Speaks the mantras with their use** — each [[mantra]] has a [[vinyog]], the statement of where and for what it is spoken. Reciting without the vinyog is reading; reciting with it is conducting.
3. **Makes the host speak the sankalpa** — the declaration of time, place, lineage and intent that opens every rite ([the sankalpa template](/reference/sankalpa/)). The host says it; the pandit prompts it word by word.
4. **Explains** — tells the family what each action means, in their own language. This is the part the yajaman remembers.
5. **Manages the rite** — the [[samagri]], the timing (a three-hour puja has a rhythm), the roles of the relatives, and the honest talk about [[dakshina]] ([the pandit's etiquette](/occasions/pandit-shishtachar/)).

Behind all five stands the ritual grammar: a small set of procedures — purification, svasti-vachana, the sankalpa, Ganesha-Ambika worship, [[kalash-sthapana]], the nine planets, the [[havan]], the aarti and the visarjana — that every occasion repeats. The [next lesson](/learn/l0-02-parampara-madhyandin/) names the tradition these come from; [Level 2](/learn/level-2/) teaches them one by one.

# Three readers, one page

Every page of this site is written for three readers at once:

- **the learner**, who wants to become a pandit and needs the order, the mantras and the reasons from the beginning — this Learn path is for you;
- **the host**, the family holding a puja at home, who wants to understand what the pandit is saying and what to keep ready — the occasion pages with their checklists are for you;
- **the practising pandit**, who wants a clean, large-type pothi on the phone that opens without the internet — the pothi mode on every occasion page is for you.

The same text serves all three because the honest description of a rite — what is done, what is said, what it means, where it is written — is what each of them needs.

# Who may learn

Tradition hands the role of the family purohit down within [[brahman]] households, from father to son and from guru to student, and the pages of this site describe who *traditionally* performs each role in each rite. Present practice is wider. [Jnana Prabodhini](https://www.jnanaprabodhini.org/paurohitya/) in Pune has trained women and men of all castes in paurohitya since 1990, and the people it trained conduct weddings, thread ceremonies and shraddhas. This site never says that anyone may not learn or perform a rite by reason of birth or sex. Where a tradition restricts a mantra — Vedic mantras with svara to initiates, for example — the site states the traditional view and its source, and presents the text for study. The Learn path is open to every reader.
