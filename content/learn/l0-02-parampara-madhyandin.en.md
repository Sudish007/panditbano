# One Veda, two branches, two recensions

Of the four Vedas, the [[yajurved]] is the Veda of the [[yajna]] — its mantras accompany ritual action. It survives in two branches. The Krishna ("black") Yajurveda — Taittiriya, Maitrayani, Katha — is recited in Maharashtra and the south. The Shukla ("white") Yajurveda, the Vajasaneyi Samhita, has two living recensions or shakhas: **Madhyandina** and **Kanva**. Their text is nearly the same; they differ in arrangement and in a few readings. The Madhyandina recension is the one recited across North India, by the Deshastha Brahmins of Maharashtra and by the Yajurvedi Shrimali Brahmins of Gujarat and Rajasthan; the Kanva recension lives in Odisha, Karnataka, Andhra, Kerala and Tamil Nadu ([Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha), [Madhyandina Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhyandina_Shakha), [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda)). The Madhyandina Samhita has forty adhyayas; the site cites it as "VS" with adhyaya and mantra number, from the text of [Sanskrit Wikisource](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B6%E0%A5%81%E0%A4%95%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B2%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%9C%E0%A5%81%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B5%E0%A5%87%E0%A4%A6%E0%A4%83) checked against the [TITUS](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/ind/aind/ved/yvw/vs/vs.htm) edition; the [Vedic Heritage Portal](https://vedicheritage.gov.in/samhitas/yajurveda/vajasneyi-madhyandina-samhita/) has recordings of its recitation.

# Why it is this site's default

Three facts decide it. First, the shakha map above: the Hindi belt recites Madhyandina. Second, the household manual: the [Paraskara Grihya Sutra](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AA%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%95%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%97%E0%A5%83%E0%A4%B9%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%AE%E0%A5%8D) belongs to the White Yajurveda and is, in Oldenberg's words, an appendix to [Katyayana's Shrauta Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalpa_%28Vedanga%29) — so the sutra that fixes the order of a Hindi-belt wedding, thread ceremony or funeral quotes exactly the mantras of this Samhita ([Oldenberg's translation, 1886](https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.89728)). Third, the handbooks the pandits actually carry — [the Gita Press paddhatis](/reference/bibliography/) and the pothis of Varanasi, Mumbai and Gorakhpur — quote Paraskara's kandikas and the Samhita's mantras page after page, and [Varanasi's Sampurnanand Sanskrit Vishwavidyalaya](https://ssvv.ac.in/programs-offered) teaches Shukla Yajurveda and Paurohitya as subjects. That is why every mantra on this site is given in its Madhyandina reading, with the readings of other recensions noted as variants where the sources differ.

# Three layers in every rite

Open any occasion page and each step carries a label: **vedic**, **paddhati** or **lokachara**.

- The **Vedic core** is the sutra's sequence with the Samhita's mantras: in the wedding, the madhuparka, the acceptance of the [[kanyadaan]], the panigrahana, the laja-homa, the [[saptapadi]], the sighting of the pole star.
- The **paddhati layer** is the Puranic frame the handbooks wrap around that core: [[ganesh-ambika-pujan]], [[kalash-sthapana]], the [[shodash-matrika]], the [[navagraha]], the [[punyahavachan]], the [[nandi-shraddha]], the [[raksha-sutra]], and the worded sankalpa of the kanyadaan. Paraskara's text has none of these; the medieval and modern compilers added them from the Puranas and the smritis.
- **Lokachara** is local and family custom — the [[tilak-ceremony]], haldi, the garland exchange, the knot of the garments, [[sindoor]], the [[mangalsutra]], kohbar, matkor. No sutra prescribes them; the family's elders do.

The site shows the three apart so that a learner knows what is Vedic, what is Puranic and what is custom — and so that no one is told that a custom is "wrong" because a sutra does not mention it. Paraskara himself gives the warrant:

# Where the regions differ

The Hindi belt is not uniform, and the site says so in every "variants" block.

- **Mithila** (north Bihar, the Nepal Terai): about three quarters of Maithil Brahmin families are Vajasaneyi and follow Madhyandina; about a quarter are Chhandog — Samavedic, of the Shandilya gotra — and their purohit follows a Samaveda household tradition, not Paraskara. The site therefore never assumes Paraskara for every Maithil family. Mithila's own sequence runs Panji verification → Siddhant → Nishchaitambul → Matkor → Parichhan or Dwarachar → Kanyadaan → **Sindoor-daan**, the central rite → Saptapadi → Kohbar → Chaturthi → Dwiragaman ([Maithil Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maithil_Brahmin)).
- **The Bhojpuri belt** (Varanasi–Gorakhpur–Ballia, west Bihar, the diaspora): the Madhyandina core with its own thick folk layer — the tilak hosted by the bride's side, the matkor where the women dig the clay with songs, the madwa with its kalash and a [Satyanarayan katha](/kathas/satyanarayan-katha/), dwar-puja, kohbar, mang-bahoran, and chhathi and barahi after a birth.
- **Rajasthan**: Madhyandina Shrimali purohits; four phere; pithi-dastur, toran-marna, hathleva; the pagri rite after a death is strongest here.
- **Punjab and Haryana**: seven phere with vows is reported; which Veda shakha the purohits follow was not established by this site's research, so its pages say "practice varies".
- **The hills**: the Kumaoni "Aanchal" wedding with suwal-pathai and dhuliarghya; Garhwal's baan and haldi-haat.
- **Awadh, Braj, Bundelkhand**: the mainstream sequence — Braj community pages describe four bhanwar, and a Bundelkhand report speaks of seven phere.

The regional appendices for each of these grow on the [wedding](/occasions/vivah/) page and its variants block; in every such block the site quotes the sutra above.

# Traditions the site labels separately

Two living reform traditions have their own paddhatis: the Arya Samaj, whose rites follow Dayananda's [Sanskar Vidhi](https://elibrary.thearyasamaj.org/book/sanskar-vidhi), and the Gayatri Pariwar, whose [Karmakand Bhaskar](https://www.awgp.org/en/literature/book/kram_kand_bhaskar/v1) and [Karmakand Pradeep](https://www.epustakalaya.com/book/karmakanda-pradeep) circulate widely online. This site describes the Pauranik Madhyandina practice of the family purohit and labels those books as what they are, without comparing or judging. A family whose purohit follows the Kanva or a Krishna Yajurveda tradition should read the site's sequences as a close relative of their own, and ask their purohit where the readings differ.
