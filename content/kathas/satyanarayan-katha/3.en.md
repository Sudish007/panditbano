## The story resumes — King Ulkamukha

At the end of the second adhyaya the rishis wanted to know who kept this [[vrat]] after the woodcutter. Suta now tells the next [[katha]]. In former times there was a king named Ulkamukha, a man of great wisdom — master of his senses, truthful, in the temple every day, and keeping the Brahmins content with daily gifts.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-1}}

His wife was lovely and devoted to him. On the bank of the river Bhadrashila the king was keeping the vrat of Satyadeva together with his wife. In the katha, Satyadeva, Satyanarayan and Prabhu (the Lord) are three names of the same god.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-3}}

## The merchant's question and his vow

Just then a merchant named Sadhu, out on a trading voyage, arrived there by boat with much wealth. "Sadhu" is the merchant's name here; the word also means "a good man", and the Hindi-belt pothis call him Sadhu the vaishya or Sadhu the baniya. He moored his boat on the bank and, seeing the king engaged in the vrat, asked humbly — O king, what is this you are doing with a heart so full of devotion? Tell me all of it.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-5}}

The king answered — worship is being offered to [[vishnu]] of matchless radiance, and this vrat is kept with my own people out of the wish for sons and the like.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-7}}

The merchant said respectfully — tell me everything and I will do just as you say; I too have no children. The sentence that follows is his own — "from this vrat a child will surely come." Such statements are spoken by the story's characters; the site shows them as [[phalashruti]], the text's own statement of its fruit.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-9}}

Back from his trading, Sadhu told his wife Lilavati everything and made a conditional [[sankalp]] — "when I have a child, then I shall perform this vrat." That one sentence is the root of the whole story that follows.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-10}}

## Kalavati is born; the vrat is put off

The text says that by the grace of Satyadeva Lilavati was expecting a child, and in the tenth month a jewel of a daughter was born. She grew day by day like the moon in the bright fortnight; at the naming rite she was called Kalavati.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-13}}

Lilavati reminded her husband in sweet words — why do you not perform the vrat you resolved upon? Sadhu put it off again — "I shall do it at the time of her wedding" — reassured his wife and left for the city. Kalavati grew up in her father's house.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-15}}

## The wedding — and the forgotten vrat

Seeing his daughter grown, among her friends, the merchant, who knew his duty, took counsel and sent a messenger — find an excellent groom for the girl. The messenger brought back a handsome, virtuous merchant's son from the city of Kanchana. Together with his kinsmen Sadhu gave his daughter to that youth with every [[vidhi]] and rule. But at the time of the wedding he forgot that excellent vrat — and at that the Lord grew angry with him.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-21}}

## A false charge in Ratnasarapura

In due course Sadhu went with his wealthy son-in-law to trade in Ratnasarapura, the lovely city of King Chandraketu by the sea. At that very time Lord Satyanarayan, seeing him fallen from his promise, laid a curse on him — a dreadful, harsh and great sorrow shall befall him.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-25}}

One day a thief who had taken the king's wealth came to the very place where the two merchants were staying. Seeing the king's men running after him, he set the wealth down there in fear and slipped away unseen. The men found the king's wealth there and took the two merchants away in bonds.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-28}}

Without any inquiry the king had the two put in the prison of the great fort. The text says that by the maya of Satyadeva nobody listened to what they said, and King Chandraketu took their wealth as well.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-31}}

## At home — Lilavati and Kalavati

Through the same curse misfortune reached their home — a thief carried off all the wealth kept in the house. Beset in mind and body, tormented by hunger and thirst and the worry of where food would come from, Lilavati wandered from house to house; the girl Kalavati too went wandering every day.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-33}}

One day, faint with hunger, Kalavati came to a [[brahman]]'s house and saw the vrat of Satyanarayan being performed there. She sat and listened to the katha, prayed for what was on her mind, took the [[prasad]] and came home at night.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-35}}

Her mother asked lovingly — daughter, where were you at night, what is on your mind? Kalavati told her at once — mother, in a Brahmin's house I saw a vrat that grants what one wishes for. This too is a character's statement, phalashruti.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-37}}

Hearing her daughter, Lilavati performed the vrat of Satyanarayan with her kinsfolk and her own people, and prayed with one clear wish — may my husband and my son-in-law come home soon, and may their offence be forgiven.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-40}}

## The king's dream and the release

Pleased by this vrat, Satyanarayan appeared to King Chandraketu in a dream — release the two imprisoned merchants in the morning and give back all their wealth; if not, I shall destroy you with your kingdom, your wealth and your sons. With these words the Lord withdrew.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-42}}

In the morning the king told his dream in the assembly and gave the order — release the two bound merchants at once. The servants freed them from their fetters and brought them before the king. The two bowed to the king, but remembering what had gone before they were too afraid to speak.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-44}}

The king spoke to them with respect — this great sorrow came upon you by fate; now there is nothing to fear. He had their fetters removed, had them shaved and bathed, and honoured them with clothes and ornaments.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-48}}

The wealth that had been taken he gave back doubled and said — Sadhu, go home to your own place. Sadhu bowed and said — by your grace we shall go. The two great merchants set out for home. Here the third adhyaya ends; the story of the voyage is in the fourth.

{{verse:satyanarayan-vrat-katha-sanskrit-3-50}}

## What to take from this adhyaya

- This is the adhyaya of the sankalpa. Sadhu's vrat was tied to a condition, "once I have a child"; when the child came it was put off to the wedding, and at the wedding it was forgotten — the katha shows what comes of that putting off and forgetting. Households still make the vow "if this works out, we will hold a Satyanarayan katha"; the practical point for the pandit is to ask the host at the sankalpa whether this katha fulfils an earlier vow, and to say that wish in the [sankalpa](/reference/sankalpa/).
- Kalavati's episode mirrors the woodcutter of the second adhyaya — sitting down at a vrat under way in someone's house, listening to the katha, praying and taking prasad. These two episodes are the basis of the custom of calling the neighbourhood to a katha.
- Lilavati performs the vrat with her kinsfolk while the men of the house are away, and prays with a specific wish. By the katha's own account anyone may host the vrat; the discussion of "who may hold it" on the [Satyanarayan puja procedure](/occasions/satyanarayan-puja/) page rests on this.
- The link between a wedding and the vrat belongs to the story. Many Hindi-belt families hold a Satyanarayan katha after a wedding and connect the custom with this episode — that is local practice; the katha itself prescribes nothing of the kind.
- The statements of fruit (verses 9, 10, 37) are spoken by the characters and marked as phalashruti. Tradition holds that this vrat frees one from trouble; the site states this as the tradition's belief and nothing more.
- King Chandraketu punishes "without any inquiry" (verse 30) — a small lesson in conduct that the story also carries: judgement without inquiry becomes injustice.
